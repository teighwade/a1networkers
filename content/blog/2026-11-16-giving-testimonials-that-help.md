Most business owners have been asked to "say a few words" about someone they have worked with. Maybe it was a Google review, a LinkedIn recommendation, or a quick testimonial at a networking meeting. And most of us have given the same answer: "They were great. Highly recommend."

That is kind, but it does not do much. Nobody reading or hearing it learns anything new, and it sounds just like every other review out there.

A strong testimonial is different. It gives the next customer a reason to pick up the phone. When you get good at giving them, you become one of the most valuable people in your network.

## Why testimonials matter so much

People trust other people more than they trust advertising. Before most customers hire a contractor, an accountant or a cleaner, they look for proof that someone like them had a good experience.

A testimonial is that proof. It also helps in quieter ways:

- **It builds the other person's confidence.** Hearing what they did well tells them what to keep doing.
- **It strengthens your relationship.** Taking the time to speak up for someone is a clear act of giving.
- **It educates the room.** When you share a testimonial at a chapter meeting, every member learns a little more about what that business does and who it serves.

That last point is a big deal in referral networking. The more your fellow members know about real jobs a business has done, the easier it is for them to spot the next opportunity.

## Start with the situation

The best testimonials follow a simple pattern: what was going on, what they did, and how it turned out.

Begin with the problem or need. "Our office printer contract was up and we had no idea what we were paying for." Or "My mom's house needed a deep clean before she moved in." This helps listeners picture their own version of the same problem.

## Be specific about what they did

This is where most testimonials fall flat. Instead of "great service," say what made it great.

- Did they call you back the same afternoon?
- Did they explain your options without pushing the expensive one?
- Did they show up on time, clean up after themselves, or find a problem you did not know about?
- Did they make a stressful situation feel easy?

One concrete detail is worth more than five adjectives. "He walked me through three options and recommended the cheapest one because it fit what I needed" tells people far more than "honest and professional."

## Share the result

Finish with how things turned out. Keep it real. You do not need dramatic numbers or big claims. "Everything has worked perfectly since" or "I have already sent my neighbor to them" is plenty.

If you can, mention how it felt to work with them. People hire businesses that make their lives easier, and saying so out loud helps the next customer imagine that same experience.

## Keep it short and honest

A spoken testimonial at a meeting should take about 30 to 60 seconds. A written review can be a few sentences. Long is not better. Clear is better.

Only share what actually happened. If you have not used someone's service yet, do not give them a testimonial. You can still help by talking about how they treat people, how reliable they are as a referral partner, or what a customer told you. Just be clear about which is which. Honest praise keeps your word valuable.

## Put it where it counts

A testimonial does the most good where future customers will see or hear it:

- **Google reviews** help local businesses show up in searches and give strangers a reason to trust them.
- **LinkedIn recommendations** carry weight for professional services like insurance, accounting, lending and real estate.
- **Facebook pages** reach friends and neighbors who already know you.
- **Chapter meetings** put the story in front of a room full of people who are actively looking for referrals.

If someone did good work for you, consider doing two of these, not just one. It takes five extra minutes and it can make a real difference for a small business.

## Make it a habit

Here is an easy goal: give one specific testimonial a month. Look back at the businesses you used recently, pick one that did a good job, and tell people about it.

Over time, this habit does something powerful. Business owners start to notice that you speak up for others, and they want to do the same for you. That is what Givers Gain® looks like in everyday practice.
