You had a great conversation over breakfast. You traded cards, you both said "let's get together," and then the week happened. A lot of networking value gets lost right there, in the gap between a good meeting and the next step.

The opposite problem is just as real. Some people follow up so hard that the other person starts dodging their calls. Nobody wants to be the person who sends a sales pitch the same afternoon they met someone.

Here is how to land in the middle: consistent, warm and useful.

## Follow up fast, but keep it light

The best time to follow up is within a day or two, while the conversation is still fresh. Your first message does not need to accomplish much. It just needs to keep the door open.

A simple note works:

> "Great meeting you Thursday. I enjoyed hearing about how you got started in landscaping. Would you be up for a coffee in the next couple of weeks so I can learn more about who you work with?"

Notice what is missing: no attachment, no price sheet, no "let me know if you need anything." It is about them, not you.

## Lead with curiosity, not a pitch

The fastest way to seem pushy is to make the follow-up about what you want. The fastest way to seem trustworthy is to make it about what they want.

Before you reach out, think about:

- What did they say they were looking for?
- Who do you know that might be a fit for them?
- Was there a question you did not get to ask?

If you can open with something useful, even a small idea or a name, you have already set yourself apart.

## Suggest a specific next step

"We should get together sometime" almost never happens. A specific invitation is easier to say yes to.

Try offering:

- **A one-to-one.** Thirty minutes over coffee to learn about each other's businesses. In BNI, this is one of the most valuable habits a member can build.
- **Two time options.** "Does Tuesday morning or Wednesday afternoon work better?" is easier than an open-ended question.
- **A place close to them.** In the West Valley, that might mean picking a spot in Surprise instead of asking someone to drive to Goodyear in June heat.

## Send what you promised

If you said you would send an article, a contact or a recommendation, send it. This is where trust gets built. People remember who actually followed through, and it costs you almost nothing.

When you make an introduction, write it so both people know why they should talk:

> "Maria, meet Dave. Dave owns a pool service company and mentioned he is looking for a reliable handyman to refer. Maria runs a handyman business in Peoria. I think you two would get along."

## Know when to step back

Not every contact turns into a relationship, and that is fine. A good rule of thumb:

1. Send your first follow-up within two days.
2. If you do not hear back, send one friendly nudge about a week later.
3. If there is still no response, let it rest. You will probably see them again at the next meeting.

Two unanswered messages are not a sign to try harder. People are busy, and summer schedules get scattered with vacations and family trips. A relaxed tone keeps the door open for later.

## Keep showing up

The easiest follow-up of all is being in the same room every week. When you see someone consistently, the relationship builds on its own. There is no need to chase anyone, because you will have dozens of small conversations over the months.

That is one of the quiet advantages of a weekly chapter. You do not have to manufacture reasons to stay in touch. Thursday morning does it for you.

## A quick follow-up checklist

- Reach out within 48 hours
- Mention something specific from your conversation
- Ask about them before talking about you
- Offer a clear next step with a time and place
- Deliver anything you promised
- Stop after two unanswered messages
