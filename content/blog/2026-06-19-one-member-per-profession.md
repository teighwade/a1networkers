One of the first things visitors hear about BNI is that a chapter allows only one member per profession. One real estate agent, one plumber, one mortgage professional, one CPA. For people used to open networking events, that can sound unusual. Once you see how a chapter works, though, the rule makes a lot of sense.

## The problem with open networking

At a typical mixer, you might meet five insurance agents and three financial planners in the same room. Everyone is competing for attention, and nobody is quite sure who to send business to. Conversations stay polite but shallow, because there is little reason to invest in each other.

BNI takes a different approach. Instead of a room full of competitors, a chapter is a team of business owners who each cover a different part of the market.

## What exclusivity does for members

When you hold the only seat for your profession, the referrals for your line of work come to you. That changes the relationship with everyone else in the room.

- **Members can recommend you with confidence.** There is no awkward choice between two people they like.
- **You can share openly.** You do not have to guard your best ideas or contacts from a competitor sitting across the table.
- **It rewards commitment.** Holding a seat is a responsibility. Members show up each week, learn about each other and work to earn the trust that comes with it.

## What it does for the chapter

The rule also helps the group as a whole. Because each seat is unique, a chapter naturally grows by adding new professions rather than more of the same. Over time that creates a well-rounded team that can handle almost any request a client brings up.

Think about a family buying a home. They might need a mortgage professional, a real estate agent, a home inspector, an insurance agent, a moving company, a cleaner and maybe a roofer or HVAC company down the line. A healthy chapter can point them to a trusted person for each step.

## Fewer competitors, more collaborators

Many chapters also look for "power teams," groups of members whose customers overlap but who do not compete. Home service providers, real estate and lending professionals, or business service providers like accountants, attorneys and marketers often refer to each other all the time. The one-per-profession rule is what makes those partnerships possible. Nobody is worried about losing a client to the person they are working with.

## How specialties are handled

Some professions are broad, so chapters sometimes divide them by specialty. For example, residential and commercial work can sometimes be treated as separate categories, depending on how different the businesses really are. The goal is to avoid direct competition while still welcoming professionals who serve distinct customers. If you are unsure whether your category fits, the best move is simply to ask.
