"Here, call this guy. He might need you."

It sounds helpful, and sometimes it is. But a name and a phone number scribbled on a napkin is usually a lead, not a referral. Understanding the difference can change how much business actually comes from your network, and how much you help the people in it.

## What a lead looks like

A lead is information about someone who might need a product or service. It usually comes with very little context:

- A name and number with no introduction
- "My neighbor is remodeling, you should reach out"
- A business you heard about that might be a fit

Leads are not bad. But when the person receiving the call has never heard of you, you are starting from scratch. It is a cold call with a little extra information.

## What a real referral looks like

A real referral is different in a few important ways:

- **The person knows you are coming.** Someone they trust has told them about you.
- **They have a need.** They are actually looking for help, not just a possible fit someday.
- **They have agreed to talk.** They expect your call or have your contact information and plan to reach out.
- **There is context.** You know a little about what they need before you start the conversation.

Think about the difference from the customer's side. A stranger calling about your kitchen is easy to ignore. A call from "the electrician my friend Lisa said would be calling" gets answered.

## Why the difference matters

Real referrals tend to close more often, move faster and come with more trust built in. The person giving the referral has already vouched for you, so a lot of the usual skepticism is gone.

That trust works both ways. When you pass a real referral, you are putting your own reputation on the line. That is a good thing. It means you only pass referrals to people you actually believe in, and it means the people receiving them know they should take good care of the person you sent.

## How to turn a lead into a referral

Most leads can become referrals with a little extra effort. Before you pass a name along, try these steps:

1. **Have a short conversation.** Ask the person a question or two about what they need.
2. **Tell them about your contact.** Explain who you know and why you trust them.
3. **Ask permission.** "Would it be OK if I had them give you a call?"
4. **Make the introduction.** A quick text or email to both people works well.
5. **Share what you learned.** Give your contact enough background to start a good conversation.

That process takes maybe five minutes, and it turns a cold call into a warm one.

## A quick example

Say a coworker mentions that their AC is struggling in the July heat.

**As a lead:** You hand your HVAC contact your coworker's number and say, "He might need you."

**As a referral:** You tell your coworker, "I know a great HVAC company. They're honest and they'll tell you if it's worth fixing. Can I have them reach out?" Then you send a group text introducing them, with a note that the system is about 14 years old and the upstairs never cools down.

Same person, same need, very different outcome.

## Be honest about what you are passing

Sometimes all you have is a lead, and that is fine. Just call it what it is. Saying "This is just a tip, they do not know you" sets the right expectation and protects trust on both sides.

## Ask for the kind you want

If you want better referrals, teach your network what a good one looks like. Be specific about who you help, what trigger to listen for and how to make the introduction. The clearer you are, the more likely people are to pass something real instead of a guess.
