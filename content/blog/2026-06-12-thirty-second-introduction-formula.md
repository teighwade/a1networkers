You have 30 seconds. The room is full of business owners who want to help you. Then you stand up, say your name, list a few services and sit down. By the time the next person finishes, nobody remembers what you said.

It happens to almost everyone at first. The good news is that a memorable introduction is not about talent. It is about structure. Here is a simple formula that works for weekly BNI presentations, chamber mixers or any time someone asks, "So what do you do?"

## Why most introductions fall flat

The most common mistakes are easy to spot:

- **Too many services.** Listing everything makes it hard to remember anything.
- **Too much jargon.** Industry terms make sense to you, not to the room.
- **No ask.** People want to help, but they need to know how.
- **Same thing every week.** If it never changes, people stop listening.

## The four-part formula

Try building your 30 seconds from these four parts.

### 1. Who you are

Your name, your business and your profession in plain words. Keep it short.

*"I'm Maria with Desert Sky Bookkeeping. I'm a bookkeeper for small businesses."*

### 2. One problem you solve

Pick one problem for this week. Describe it the way a customer would describe it, not the way you would.

*"A lot of business owners I meet are three months behind on their books and dreading tax time."*

### 3. A quick picture

Give the room something to see. A short example, a before-and-after or a common situation helps people connect the dots.

*"Last month I helped a landscaper get caught up in two weeks so he could finally see which jobs were actually making money."*

Keep it general enough that you are not sharing anyone's private details, and only use examples that are true.

### 4. A specific ask

Tell the room exactly who you want to meet and how to bring you up.

*"This week I'm looking for owners of service businesses with two to ten employees. If you hear someone complain about their books, ask if they'd like a quick call with me."*

That is it. Four parts, about 30 seconds, and the room walks away knowing exactly who to send your way.

## Make it easy to remember

A few small habits make your introduction stick:

- **Use a memory hook.** A short, consistent line you say every week, like a tagline. Something tied to your business works best.
- **Speak slowly.** Thirty seconds feels short, but rushing makes it feel shorter.
- **Stand still and look up.** Reading from your phone loses the room.
- **Say your name twice.** Once at the start, once at the end.

## Rotate your focus

The fastest way to teach a room about your business is to cover one piece at a time. Make a list of your main services or customer types and rotate through them week by week.

Seasonal angles help too. In June in the West Valley, a lot of businesses have a natural summer story. A pool service talks about algae as the water warms up. An HVAC company talks about systems working hard in triple-digit heat. A roofer talks about getting ready before monsoon storms arrive. Tying your ask to what people are already thinking about makes it easier to act on.

## Practice out loud

Write it down, then say it out loud and time it. You will probably be over. Cut words until it fits comfortably with a breath to spare.

Then practice a few more times. You do not need to memorize every word, but you should know your four parts well enough that you can deliver them without notes.

## Measure what works

Pay attention to what happens after you speak. Did anyone ask a follow-up question? Did someone mention a name? Did a referral come in that week? Over time you will notice which asks get a reaction and which ones do not. Keep the ones that work and rework the rest.
